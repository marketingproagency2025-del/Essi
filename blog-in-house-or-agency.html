The Question Is Not Who Posts. It Is Who Answers.

The decision usually gets framed as a cost comparison. An agency retainer against a junior salary, or against the hours somebody already on the payroll would spend. Framed that way, in-house nearly always looks cheaper, and quite often it genuinely is.

But the comparison is measuring the wrong thing. Posting is the visible part of social media and the smallest part of the work. What decides whether any of it earns money is what happens after somebody responds, and that is the part almost nobody budgets for in either direction.

The Short Answer

Keep it in-house when the product is visual, the knowledge lives with your own people, and one named person genuinely owns the job rather than adding it to a list.

Hire an agency when posting has to turn into enquiries, when nobody in the building has time to chase those enquiries the same day, or when you need paid campaigns running alongside the organic posting and nobody wants to learn Ads Manager.

If you are choosing purely because in-house is cheaper, be honest about which of the two it is cheaper than: a full service, or an account that goes quiet in November.

What In-House Is Genuinely Better At

Access, and it is not close. Somebody standing in the workshop can film a delivery arriving, a fitting going wrong, a product going out of the door. No agency on a monthly visit will ever match that, and it is the material that actually performs, because it looks like a real business rather than a brochure.

Product knowledge is the second. Your people can answer a technical comment in the right words without checking with anybody, and the answer will be correct. That builds more trust in a comment thread than any amount of polish.

Speed on small things, third. A price change, a correction, a reply to an unhappy customer, a story about something that happened an hour ago. All of that moves faster when the person holding the phone also works there.

Anyone telling you in-house cannot work is selling something. It works well, and it works for a long time, right up until the point below.

Where In-House Usually Breaks

It is the first thing dropped in a busy week. Social media has no deadline that anyone outside the company notices, so it loses every collision with a customer, an order or a problem. The failure mode is not bad posting. It is three weeks of nothing, then an apology post.

Nobody owns the measurement. Posting continues, and the question of whether it produced anything quietly stops being asked, because the person doing it was never given a number to hit.

The ad account is the sharpest edge. Organic posting is forgiving and paid is not. Money leaves the account whether or not the structure underneath it makes sense, and an untrained hand can spend a meaningful budget on the wrong objective for months without anything visibly breaking.

And it stops for holidays, illness and resignations. The person who did the posting leaves, and the passwords, the drafts and the plan leave with them.

What You Are Actually Buying From an Agency

Not posts. If the entire proposal is a number of posts per month, you are being sold the cheapest part of the job and it is fair to say so.

What is worth paying for is a system that continues when everyone is busy: a plan that exists in writing, production that is booked rather than improvised, publishing that happens on the days it said it would, paid campaigns run by somebody who does it every day, and a defined route from a comment or a form to a phone call.

That last item is the one that separates agencies from each other. Ask what happens to an enquiry in the first hour, and listen for whether the answer involves a person or a spreadsheet.

You are also buying the absence of a single point of failure. Nobody resigning takes the account with them.

The Hybrid Most Businesses Land On

It is usually the right answer and it rarely gets proposed, because neither side benefits from suggesting it. You shoot, we run.

The material comes from inside, where the access is, often just from a phone. The planning, the editing, the writing, the paid campaigns and the follow-up sit outside, where the time and the practice are. Somebody in the business is asked for footage, not for a strategy.

This tends to survive a busy month, because the only in-house obligation is small and concrete. It also costs less than a full-service arrangement while keeping the part that produces enquiries.

The version that fails is the reverse: an agency producing the content and the business handling the leads whenever it can. That puts the fragile half in the place with the least time.

Questions Worth Asking Before You Commit to Either

Ask an agency what happens to an enquiry in the first hour, who makes the call, and in what language. If nobody calls, you are buying content and keeping the hard part.

Ask what you will be shown at 30 days, and whether it includes anything past reach and engagement. Reach is not a result, it is a precondition for one.

Ask who owns the ad account and the page. It should be you, in your own business manager, with the agency holding access. This matters only once, on the day the relationship ends, and by then it is too late to arrange.

And ask the same of yourself before going in-house: which named person owns this, what are they being measured on, how quickly can they ring somebody who filled in a form on a Friday afternoon, and what happens to all of it while they are away for two weeks.

The Short Version

  • Posting is the visible part of the job and the smallest part. The decision should turn on who answers, not who posts.
  • In-house genuinely wins on access, product knowledge and speed, and anyone denying that is selling something.
  • In-house reliably breaks on busy weeks, unmeasured results, the ad account and staff leaving.
  • An agency worth paying is selling a system and a route from enquiry to phone call, not a number of posts per month.
  • The hybrid works best: your people shoot, an outside team plans, runs the ads and does the follow-up.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is an agency cheaper than hiring someone in-house?

Usually, once you count properly. A single hire costs the salary plus the tools, the training and the cover while they are away, and one person rarely covers content, paid campaigns and follow-up to a good standard. But a cheap agency retainer that only produces posts is not cheaper than a good in-house owner, so compare like with like.

Can we start in-house and move to an agency later?

Yes, and it is a sensible order. Running it yourself for a few months teaches you what your audience responds to and gives any agency real material to start from. Keep the page and the ad account in your own business manager from the first day so that the handover is an access change rather than a rebuild.

What should an agency show us after the first month?

How many enquiries arrived, what each one cost, how quickly they were contacted and how many turned into real conversations. Reach and follower growth can be in the report, but they should not be the headline. If the first report is only reach and engagement, ask what the plan is for the second.
